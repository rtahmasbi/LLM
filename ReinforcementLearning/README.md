## `DPOTrainer` - Direct Preference Optimization
```json
{
    "prompt": "...",
    "chosen": "...",   # preferred answer
    "rejected": "..."  # dispreferred answer
}
```
DPO fine-tunes a model by increasing the likelihood of the chosen output relative to rejected output.
```math
A_\theta(x, c) = \log \pi_\theta(c \mid x) - \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(c \mid x),
```

```math
A_\theta(x, r) = \log \pi_\theta(r \mid x) - \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(r \mid x).
```

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}} = - \log \sigma\!\left( \beta \left[ A_\theta(x, c) - A_\theta(x, r) \right] \right)
```

- The IPO authors provide a deeper theoretical understanding of the DPO algorithms and identify an issue with overfitting and propose an alternative loss which can be used via the loss_type="ipo" argument to the trainer.

## `CPOTrainer` - Contrastive Preference Optimization
CPO aims to mitigate two fundamental shortcomings of SFT. First, SFT’s methodology of minimizing the discrepancy between predicted outputs and gold-standard references inherently caps model performance at the quality level of the training data. Secondly, SFT lacks a mechanism to prevent the model from rejecting mistakes in translations. The CPO objective is derived from the DPO objective.



## `PPOTrainer` - Proximal Policy Optimisation
PPO uses a clipped policy gradient objective + KL reward shaping.

The clip function is defined as: $$clip(x,a,b)$$ is $$a$$ if $$x<a$$, is $$b$$ if $$x>b$$ else, $$x$$.


Policy ratio:

$$r_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t \mid s_t)}.$$

Clipped PPO Objective:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{PPO}} = \mathbb{E}_t [\min( r_t(\theta) A_t, \text{clip}(r_t(\theta), 1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon) A_t )].$$


KL Penalty (used in RLHF):

$$r_t^{\text{RLHF}} = r_t^{\text{RM}} - \beta \, D_{\mathrm{KL}}
(
\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s_t)
\;\|\;
\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot \mid s_t) ).
$$


Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE):

$$A_t = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} (\gamma \lambda)^l (\delta_{t+l}), \qquad
\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t).
$$


Full PPO Loss (Policy + Value + Entropy):

$$\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{PPO}} + c_v \, (V_\theta(s_t) - R_t)^2 - c_e \, H(\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s_t)).
$$

## `GRPOTrainer` - Group Relative Policy Optimization
GRPO is an online learning algorithm, meaning it improves iteratively by using the data generated by the trained model itself during training. The intuition behind GRPO objective is to maximize the advantage of the generated completions, while ensuring that the model remains close to the reference policy.

## `KTOTrainer` - Kahneman-Tversky Optimization
Kahneman-Tversky Optimization (KTO) is a training method designed to align a language model using only a binary signal of whether an output is desirable or undesirable for a given input, rather than pairs of preferred/dispreferred completions. Drawing on the prospect theory of Kahneman and Tversky, it defines a human-aware loss (HALO) that directly maximizes the utility of generations, weighting desirable and undesirable examples asymmetrically to reflect human loss aversion.

# Terms
## Rollout
A rollout is one complete run of the policy (the model/agent) acting in its environment, from start to finish, that produces the trajectory you'll compute a reward for.

- In a general RL sense: a rollout is the sequence of states, actions, and outcomes generated when you let the current policy interact with the environment — e.g., a robot policy taking actions until an episode ends.
- In an LLM/agent RL sense: a rollout is one full attempt by the agent at a task — the model reads the prompt/task, produces a sequence of actions (write code, call a tool, run a compiler, revise), and the process runs until it produces a final output or hits a stop condition.
- In the hardware-verification: a rollout would be one full attempt where the agent writes a testbench or proposes an RTL fix, that gets run through the simulator, and the simulator's result becomes the raw material for computing reward.


One rollout in practice = one row of training data once it's been scored: (task, actions taken, final output, reward). A training step consumes a batch of these.



## Staleness
Staleness is the gap between the policy that generated a piece of data (a rollout, an action) and the current policy being trained.

Here's the concrete picture:

1. At training step 100, the trainer has policy checkpoint $\pi_{100}$. It sends this checkpoint out to a fleet of workers to generate rollouts.
2. Some rollouts take 30 seconds (fast simulator run), others take 20 minutes (slow one).
3. By the time the 20-minute rollout finishes, the trainer has already done more gradient updates — it's now on policy checkpoint $\pi_{108}$.
4. That slow rollout was generated by $\pi_{100}$, but it's arriving to be used for training against a policy that's already 8 steps newer. The action it took, the choices it made — those reflect an "old" version of the policy that no longer exists.


**Why it matters**: RL algorithms like PPO assume (to varying degrees) that the data you're learning from was generated by the policy you're currently updating — that's what "on-policy" means. If you feed in data from a policy that's meaningfully different from the current one, the gradient you compute can point in a somewhat wrong direction, or the statistics (like the importance-sampling ratio) become unreliable.

Staleness = "how many policy versions old is this piece of data." A staleness of 0 means the rollout was generated by the exact current policy (fully synchronous, on-policy). A staleness of 8 means it was generated 8 policy-updates ago.

Why **async training** creates this problem on purpose: if you forced zero staleness, you'd have to wait for every single rollout — including the slow 20-minute ones — before doing any update. That wastes the GPUs. So async designs tolerate some staleness in exchange for keeping the pipeline full, and then manage the risk with:
- Staleness caps — discard anything too old
- Importance sampling correction — the PPO clip ratio partially compensates for it
- Down-weighting — give stale data less influence on the gradient than fresh data

It's the core tradeoff of the whole async design: staleness is the "cost" you accept in exchange for not idling on the slowest worker in the batch.



## The synchronous and asynchronous
`GRPOTrainer` vs `AsyncGRPOTrainer` for example.

Standard PPO: collect a batch of N rollouts from the current policy, wait for all N to finish, compute advantages, do a fixed number of gradient update epochs on that batch, then throw the batch away and repeat. The policy used to generate the batch and the policy being updated are always in sync — that's what makes it "on-policy."

The problem in your setup: if rollout latency ranges from 30 seconds to 20 minutes, "wait for all N to finish" means your GPUs sit idle for 20 minutes waiting on the slowest simulator run in the batch. That's the whole motivation for going async.

**What "async" actually changes**

Instead of batch-and-wait, you run a continuous stream: workers keep generating rollouts from whatever policy checkpoint they currently have, and completed rollouts get pushed into a queue/buffer. The trainer consumes whatever's in the buffer at each step and updates the policy — it doesn't wait for a specific set of rollouts to complete.

This introduces policy staleness: a rollout being scored now might have been generated by a policy checkpoint that's already several gradient steps old, because it was one of the slow ones. You're no longer strictly on-policy.

**How you correct for staleness**

- Importance sampling ratio: PPO already computes a ratio π_new(a|s) / π_old(a|s) for its clipped objective. In the async setting, π_old is literally the policy that generated the action (which could be several steps stale), not just "yesterday's checkpoint." As long as you log which policy version generated each rollout, the existing PPO clipping mechanism does most of the work of tolerating this — the clip range (typically ε=0.2) bounds how much a stale, off-policy sample can move the gradient.
- Staleness cap: in practice, most systems set a hard limit — discard or down-weight rollouts generated by a policy checkpoint more than K steps old, since the importance ratio becomes unreliable/high-variance past a certain staleness.
- GRPO simplifies this somewhat: instead of a learned value function baseline, GRPO computes advantage by normalizing reward within a group of rollouts sampled for the same prompt/task (reward minus group mean, divided by group std). This means you need several rollouts per task to land roughly together in time to form a group — which actually reintroduces a soft synchronization requirement even in an otherwise async system: you don't need the whole global batch to finish, but you do need each task's rollout group to complete before you can compute that group's advantages.

**The practical architecture this leads to**

- A policy version counter — every rollout is tagged with the checkpoint ID that generated it.
- A replay/streaming buffer that groups rollouts by task (for GRPO) and tracks staleness.
- The trainer pulls "ready" groups (all rollouts in the group complete, staleness under threshold) rather than a fixed global batch size.
- Periodic checkpoint push to rollout workers — not every step, since redistributing a fresh policy to hundreds of workers has its own cost/latency, so there's a tradeoff between staleness and checkpoint-sync overhead.





## Staged rewards
Your reward pipeline isn't one grader call, but multiple stages (cheap check → proxy grader → expensive/ground-truth grader), which is exactly the two-tier structure we built for the LLM-grader curveball, just with their exact name for it.


## Proxy graders
Cheaper/faster stand-ins for the real (expensive, slow) reward signal — e.g., a fast heuristic or smaller model that approximates what a full simulation run would tell you, used to get a reward estimate before the real result lands.

This is a staged-reward design almost by definition. You don't wait for the expensive grader before the policy can use some signal:
- Stage 1 (fast, always runs): compile/run-success check — free, near-instant, filters obvious failures.
- Stage 2 (proxy grader): a fast approximate reward — a smaller model or heuristic — attached to the rollout as soon as it's available. Uncertainty-aware: the proxy grader outputs a confidence alongside the score.
- Stage 3 (expensive grader/simulator ground truth): runs asynchronously in the background, only gets prioritized for low-confidence Stage 2 outputs (sliced evaluation path — route by proxy confidence).


## Sliced evaluation paths
Routing different rollouts through different evaluation pipelines depending on characteristics (e.g., simple cases get the fast path, ambiguous or high-stakes ones get the full expensive path).

## Uncertainty-aware evaluation
The grader should report a confidence, not just a score, so the system knows when to trust a proxy grader's output vs. escalate to a more expensive check. This is a step beyond what I described — worth having a specific answer ready for it.

## Retry strategies
Explicit handling for when a rollout, a sim, or a grader call fails or times out (retry with backoff, retry with reduced fidelity, or discard and resample).

Retry strategy: if the expensive grader times out or the simulator crashes, don't block the policy update on it — retry with backoff a bounded number of times, then fall back to the proxy score with a flagged "unverified" tag so it can be down-weighted or excluded from high-confidence training statistics.


## ROCm
ROCm (Radeon Open Compute) is AMD's open-source software stack for running GPU-accelerated computing and AI workloads on AMD hardware — it's the direct counterpart to NVIDIA's CUDA.

**Core pieces**:
- HIP (Heterogeneous-compute Interface for Portability) — the programming layer at the center of ROCm. It's a C++ dialect that lets you write GPU code once and run it on either AMD or NVIDIA hardware with minimal changes.
- HIPify — a conversion tool that translates existing CUDA C++ code into HIP, used when porting CUDA codebases to run on AMD GPUs.
- Compilers, math/deep-learning libraries, profilers, and debugging tools that mirror what CUDA provides on the NVIDIA side.

**How it compares to CUDA right now**: CUDA typically outperforms ROCm by 10-30%, while ROCm costs 15-40% less but requires more technical expertise. PyTorch officially supports ROCm now, though CUDA still has broader framework compatibility. AMD has been closing the gap — at CES 2026, AMD rolled out ROCm 7.2 with expanded platform support (including Windows, historically a Linux-only stack) and significant performance gains, especially for AI workloads. 



## Question 1
You're building the RL training infrastructure for an agent that iteratively improves at a hardware verification task (e.g., writing testbenches or fixing RTL bugs). Each rollout requires running a simulator, which can take anywhere from 30 seconds to 20 minutes depending on the design. You need to run thousands of rollouts per training step, score them, and feed rewards back into the policy update.

1. Orchestration & scheduling

Use a job queue (Kubernetes Jobs or a custom scheduler on top of Slurm/Ray) with a pool of simulator workers. Treat each rollout as an independent task pulled from a queue — don't pre-assign rollouts to fixed workers, so faster workers naturally pick up more work.
Decouple rollout generation from policy update: an async/off-policy setup (like a replay buffer or streaming reward queue) so the trainer doesn't block waiting for the slowest simulation in a batch.
Autoscale the worker pool based on queue depth; use spot/preemptible compute for the simulator fleet since individual rollouts are cheap to retry.

2. Long-tail latency

Set a per-rollout timeout budget with early termination — if a sim is way past the expected time for its design class, kill it and either retry with reduced fidelity or discard.
Use asynchronous PPO/GRPO-style training where the policy updates on whatever batch of completed rollouts is available at step boundary, rather than waiting for a fixed-size synchronous batch. This is the standard fix for heterogeneous rollout latency in RL infra.
Bucket tasks by expected runtime (from historical data) so scheduling can pack a good mix of fast/slow jobs per worker rather than randomly stalling a worker on a 20-minute job.

3. Reward/grading pipeline

Separate the grader into its own service, versioned independently from the policy — you want to be able to fix a grader bug without retraining from scratch.
Multi-signal reward: correctness (did the testbench catch the bug / does RTL pass verification) as the primary signal, plus auxiliary signals (compile success, sim didn't crash, code complexity) to shape reward and reduce sparsity.
Cache grading results keyed by rollout output hash — verification runs are expensive, don't regrade identical outputs.

4. Detecting reward hacking / broken evals

Track reward distribution over time — a sudden spike in average reward without a corresponding jump in a held-out human-graded sample is a red flag.
Maintain a small, frozen "golden set" of known-correct and known-broken solutions; periodically run the grader against it to catch silent grader regressions.
Log the actual rollout transcripts/outputs, not just scores — spot-check periodically for degenerate strategies (e.g., agent found a way to make the simulator report success without a real fix).

5. Logging/monitoring

Per-rollout: latency, exit status, reward breakdown (not just total), simulator version/config used.
Per-training-step: reward mean/variance, KL divergence from reference policy, gradient norms, fraction of rollouts timed out or failed.
Dashboards correlating reward trends with infra health (queue depth, worker failure rate) — a lot of "the model got worse" bugs are actually "the infra silently started dropping rollouts."






## question 2
What if your grader itself is an LLM and it's slow/expensive?

1. Cost/latency problem first

An LLM grader is now itself a rollout-scale bottleneck — you're paying inference cost + latency for every single rollout, potentially thousands per step. Treat grading as its own scalable service, not an inline call: batch grading requests, use a smaller/distilled grader model for the bulk pass, and reserve the expensive frontier-model grader for a sampled subset or for cases the cheap grader is uncertain about.

2. Two-tier grading

Cheap heuristic/rule-based checks first (did it compile, did the sim run, basic structural checks) to filter out obviously-bad rollouts for free.
Small/fast LLM grader for the bulk of what's left.
Expensive LLM grader (or human) only on a sampled slice, used to calibrate and validate the cheap grader rather than grade everything — this is also your defense against grader drift.

3. Caching and determinism

LLM graders are often non-deterministic — same output can get different scores on different calls. Either pin temperature to 0 for grading, or average over a few calls and treat variance itself as a signal (high variance = ambiguous case, maybe route to human review).
Cache aggressively by rollout-output hash, since retraining often regrades near-duplicate outputs across steps.

4. Latency decoupling

Since LLM grading adds its own delay on top of simulator latency, this reinforces the case for async/off-policy training even more strongly — you don't want the trainer blocked on a grading queue on top of a simulation queue. Reward arrives whenever it arrives; the policy update consumes what's ready.

5. Grader reliability as its own eval problem

The LLM grader needs the same "golden set" regression testing as any other component — periodically check it against known-good/known-bad examples, because LLM grader behavior can silently drift with model updates, prompt changes, or even just API version bumps.




## Curveball 1: "Your reward is going up steadily, but a spot-check shows the agent's actual outputs are garbage. Walk me through how you'd debug this."

Think about: is this a grader bug, a reward-hacking exploit, a distribution shift between training and eval, or a logging bug that's misreporting reward? What's your first check, second check, third check — in what order and why?


Order of investigation, cheapest/most-likely-culprit first:

1. Check the grader before anything else. This is the single most common cause. Pull the golden set, rerun it through the current grader version — if it's scoring known-bad examples as good, you've found it (grader drift, a bad prompt change, an API model swap, a bug in a recent grader deploy).
2. Check for reward hacking. Read raw rollout transcripts, not scores. Look for degenerate patterns — e.g., agent found a way to make the simulator report a false pass, or is exploiting a scoring edge case (padding output to trigger a length bonus, etc.).
3. Check for train/eval distribution shift. Is the agent overfitting to quirks of the training task distribution that don't reflect the spot-check set? Compare reward on training-distribution held-out set vs. the spot-check sample.
4. Check the logging pipeline itself. Is the number labeled "reward" in your dashboard actually the reward being fed to the policy update, or is there a stale/duplicated metric being plotted? Verify by tracing one specific rollout end-to-end through the pipeline.



## Curveball 2: "Leadership wants training throughput 3x'd by next week. You can't get more GPUs. What do you do?"

Think about: where's the actual bottleneck likely to be (simulator fleet vs. GPU-bound policy update vs. grading), and how do you even find out before proposing a fix? This is testing whether you reach for profiling before guessing.

1. Profile before proposing anything. Figure out the actual bottleneck: is training GPU-bound (policy forward/backward), or is the pipeline actually bottlenecked on the simulator fleet or the grader? In most agentic-RL setups like this, GPUs sit idle a lot waiting on simulation/grading — so the "no more GPUs" constraint may not even be the binding one.
2. If simulator-bound: reduce simulation cost — lower-fidelity sims for early training, caching identical outputs, killing long-tail outlier runs (from earlier), or increasing the simulator worker pool (CPU machines are usually cheaper/easier to add than GPUs).
3. If GPU-bound: mixed precision, larger batch sizes with gradient accumulation, better GPU utilization (are you actually saturating them, or is there idle time from synchronization stalls), reducing model size for the rollout/inference side vs. the training side (smaller inference-time policy checkpoint synced periodically).
4. If grader-bound (LLM grader): apply the two-tier grading from earlier — push more load onto the cheap grader tier.



## Curveball 3: "Two rollout workers report different rewards for what looks like the identical output. Is this a bug?"

Think about: sources of legitimate non-determinism (simulator randomness, LLM grader sampling, floating-point differences across hardware) vs. actual bugs (race condition, stale cache, versioning mismatch). How would you tell which it is?


Systematic elimination, not a guess:

1. Check grader determinism first. If the grader is an LLM, non-zero temperature alone explains this — verify by checking if reward variance correlates with anything using an LLM grader vs. deterministic checks.
2. Check for a caching/versioning mismatch. Are both workers running the same grader version and the same simulator version/config? A stale cache entry or an unpinned dependency is a classic silent culprit here.
3. Check simulator non-determinism. Some simulators have inherent randomness (uninitialized memory, race conditions in the sim itself, floating-point non-associativity across hardware) — legitimate, but you should quantify how much variance is "normal" so you can flag outliers.
4. Rule out an actual race condition in your own pipeline — e.g., a shared mutable state object being read by two workers concurrently, or output overwritten before grading.



## PPO / DPO / GRPO tradeoffs

**PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization)**

Needs a learned value function (critic) to compute advantages, plus the policy (actor) — so you're training two networks.
Uses a clipped surrogate objective to prevent the policy from moving too far in one update, which is what makes it stable enough to be the long-standing RLHF standard.
Downside: the critic is expensive to train well, especially for LLMs where reward is sparse/delayed — a bad value function gives you noisy advantage estimates and unstable training.

**DPO (Direct Preference Optimization)**

Skips RL entirely — reformulates the preference-learning objective as a simple classification-style loss directly on pairs of (preferred, rejected) outputs, using the reference policy as an implicit reward baseline.
Big tradeoff: no rollouts, no reward model, no online sampling — much simpler and cheaper to train. But it's inherently offline — you're learning from a fixed preference dataset, so it can't adapt to the current policy's actual failure modes the way an online method can, and it doesn't naturally extend to multi-turn or tool-use settings where reward isn't just "which of these two outputs is better."

**GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization)**

Drops the learned critic entirely. Instead, for a given prompt/task, sample a group of rollouts, then compute each one's advantage as its reward normalized against the group's mean/std.
Tradeoff: cheaper (no critic to train, no critic-induced bias), and it's a natural fit for verifiable-reward domains (math, code, hardware verification) where you can generate many attempts at the same task cheaply. Downside: needs multiple rollouts per task to form a meaningful group, which increases sampling cost per training example, and the group-normalized advantage is noisier with small group sizes.



## Reward modeling and reward hacking

**Detecting it**:

- Reward-vs-quality divergence: track reward alongside a small held-out human-graded or golden-set sample. If reward keeps climbing while golden-set performance plateaus or drops, that's the clearest signal.
- Transcript spot-checks: periodically read raw rollout outputs, not just scores — hacking often shows up as degenerate patterns (gaming a length bonus, outputs that satisfy the letter of the grader but not the actual task).
- Reward distribution shape: sudden reward spikes concentrated in a narrow band, or a cluster of identical/near-identical outputs getting max reward, is a red flag.
- KL divergence from the reference policy: if it's spiking, the policy is moving far from its starting behavior, which raises the odds it found an exploit rather than genuinely improving.

**Mitigating it**:

- Reward shaping / multi-signal reward: don't rely on one signal that's easy to game — combine correctness with auxiliary checks (did it compile, does it pass a held-out test set, resource usage) so gaming one dimension doesn't max out total reward.
- Golden-set regression testing on the grader itself: the grader is as much a target for exploits as the reward — verify it against known-good/known-bad examples periodically.
- KL penalty / trust region: constrain how far the policy can move per step (this is literally what PPO's clip and the KL term in RLHF do) — limits how aggressively a policy can chase an exploit before you notice.
- Adversarial/red-team the reward function before deployment: have researchers specifically try to find exploits, the same way you'd pen-test a system.



## Sampling strategies — exploration vs. exploitation at scale
- Temperature/top-p tuning: higher temperature during rollout generation increases diversity (exploration) at the cost of more low-quality samples to filter out; lower temperature during eval/deployment favors exploitation of what the policy already knows works.
- Best-of-N / self-consistency sampling: generate N rollouts per task and pick the best (or majority vote), trading compute for quality — common in GRPO-style setups where you need a group anyway.
- Curriculum/task-difficulty scheduling: don't sample uniformly across all tasks — bias sampling toward tasks near the policy's current ability frontier (too easy = no learning signal, too hard = no successful rollouts to learn from).
- At scale, the real constraint becomes compute allocation: do you spend a fixed rollout budget on more samples per task (deeper exploration on fewer tasks) or fewer samples across more tasks (broader coverage)? This is a direct tradeoff interviewers may probe — there's no universally right answer, but you should be able to reason about it: broad coverage helps generalization, concentrated sampling helps squeeze signal out of hard/rare tasks.
- Entropy bonus: adding a small entropy term to the objective discourages the policy from collapsing to a single deterministic output too early, preserving exploration during training even as the policy sharpens.
